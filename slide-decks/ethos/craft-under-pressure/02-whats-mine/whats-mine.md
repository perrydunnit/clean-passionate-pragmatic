## Since We Last Met
### What happened to the sentence?

Last time everyone left with one thing to describe accurately, to one person who did not already know it.

<div class="questions">
<p>Who did you tell, and what did it cost?</p>
<p>What did the room do — argue, shrug, agree, or simply not care?</p>
</div>

Note:
This is the most important five minutes of the session, because it is the evidence everything else rests on. Almost everybody's answer is: it cost less than I expected.

Take three or four stories before moving on, and ask the follow-up that matters: *did anything change?* Usually nothing changed, and that is worth naming as a result rather than a failure. The sentence was still correct, and being correct without consequences is exactly the state this session is trying to make available.

If somebody reports it going badly, take that seriously and say so out loud. It is the honest counter-example and the room will trust you more for handling it.

## Reading Assignment
### Three short books, all finishable

<div class="case">
<p><strong>Attendees.</strong> The <i>Enchiridion</i> of Epictetus, in full — about an hour. Then <i>Tao Te Ching</i> ch. 8, 22, 43, 78, and <i>The Art of War</i> ch. 1, 3, 10.</p>
<p><strong>Presenters.</strong> Add <i>The Heart of the Buddha's Teaching</i>, ch. 9 and 12.</p>
</div>

Nothing here is about software. That is the point: these are older answers to a question the industry has not answered.

Note:
This is the session where the reading is genuinely doable, and it is worth saying so plainly, because the series has a reputation by now for a reading load nobody clears.

The *Enchiridion* is the one to finish. It is short, it is a manual rather than a treatise, and it is the best return on an hour anywhere in this collection.

The other three are here because they say the same idea in different registers, and different registers reach different people. Nobody needs all four; they need whichever one they will not resist.

# What's Mine <small>Control, force, and where the energy goes</small>

<div class="hairline"></div>

Note:
Last session was diagnosis. This one is about the only part of the situation that is actually yours — smaller than most people expect, and more useful than it sounds.

Give the room fair warning at the start: this session can be heard as advice to accept a bad situation. It is not, and the last third of the deck is the argument against that reading. Say that now, so nobody spends the hour defending against a message you are not sending.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Enchiridion §1, §5, §8" -->

## What is up to you
### Epictetus, <i>Enchiridion</i>

<div class="quote">
<p>Some things are in our power, and others are not.</p>
<cite>Epictetus, <i>Enchiridion</i>, §1</cite>
</div>

Two consequences follow, both in the same short book. Distress comes from your judgment about an event rather than from the event itself (§5). And wanting events to be other than they are is the whole of the work (§8).

Note:
Read §1 and stop. It is a few words of substance and it reorganises everything after it.

The common misreading is that this is a claim about how much control you have. It is not a claim about amount; it is a claim about kind. There are things you decide, things you influence, and things you are merely adjacent to — and the mistake that produces most workplace misery is treating the third as though it were the first.

§5 sounds like a slogan until you use it. Take a concrete case from the room — last week's stress — and split it: what was the event, and what was the opinion about the event. The ratio is usually embarrassing.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Enchiridion §16" -->

## Empathy without absorption
### The section everybody skips

When someone is in distress, you are asked to feel it with them and not to be carried away by it.

The distinction is not coldness. It is the difference between being useful in the room and becoming a second person who needs help.

Note:
§16 is the most practically useful page in the book and the one nobody quotes, because it is not aphoristic.

The scene Epictetus describes is somebody grieving: sympathise outwardly, and do not groan inwardly. Be present without joining. In a workplace the scene is a colleague panicking about a release, and the failure mode is identical — you catch the panic, and now there are two people who cannot think.

This matters for the room specifically. Many people here are the ones others come to with problems, and absorbing every problem is the fastest route back to the exhaustion session 1 described. Staying calm is not a lesser contribution than caring most.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Tao Te Ching 8, 22, 43, 78" -->

## Water, not force

<div class="quote">
<p>Nothing in the world is as soft and yielding as water, and nothing wears away the hard and strong so well.</p>
<cite>Laozi, <i>Tao Te Ching</i>, 78</cite>
</div>

Yielding is not capitulation. It is the only strategy that survives contact with something you cannot move — a choice about *where* force goes, not a decision to stop caring.

Note:
This is the slide most likely to be misunderstood, and the room will tell you so. Let them. The objection — "so I should just go along with it?" — is the right objection, and it deserves a real answer rather than a defence of the tradition.

The answer is the second paragraph. Water does not decline to act; it takes the only route still open and keeps going. What it does not do is spend its entire volume against the one boulder it cannot move.

Offer the concrete version: which of last week's frustrations did you push against repeatedly, already knowing the answer would not change? That is the energy this is about.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Right view, right effort" -->

## Right view, right effort
### Thich Nhat Hanh, ch. 9 and 12

**Right view** is seeing the situation as it is, without the extra layer of story about what it means and who is to blame.

**Right effort** is spending energy where it can actually move something. Anxiety is usually effort spent on the part you cannot touch.

Note:
Two chapters, one idea — and the framing is useful even for people with no interest in Buddhism: the story layer is optional, and the effort is usually misdirected.

Right view is not optimism and not detachment. It is accuracy — the same accuracy the carry-forward asked for last week, applied inward. The story is where "this always happens to me" and "they are doing this deliberately" live, and both cost energy while producing nothing.

Right effort is the bridge to the next slide. Ask the room to name one worry from the past week and say which of the two it belongs to.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Art of War 1, 3, 10" -->

## Read the terrain
### Sun Tzu, ch. 1, 3, 10

Before committing energy, know the ground you are actually standing on — its constraints, its bottlenecks, its real condition — rather than the ground you were promised.

These chapters are about situational awareness and conservation of strength. They are not about manoeuvring against colleagues, and no reading of them makes a coworker an enemy.

Note:
Say the second paragraph out loud before anyone else does. Somebody will hear the word "war" and stop listening, and the book is worth rescuing from that reaction, because its actual content is unglamorous: look at the ground, count your supplies, do not fight where you cannot win.

The workplace translation is unromantic and useful. The terrain is what the system actually does, not what the roadmap says it will do. Reading it means checking real deployment times, real failure rates, real time-to-review — the things that decide what is possible next month regardless of what anyone has said.

The conservation point connects to water: strength spent on an unwinnable argument is not merely wasted, it is unavailable for the argument you could have won.

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="The three filters" -->

## The three filters
### Before you spend it

<div class="questions">
<p><strong>Is this mine to decide?</strong> If not, you are allowed an opinion and not much else.</p>
<p><strong>Would saying it change anything?</strong> Not "would it be true" — would it move.</p>
<p><strong>Does the saying cost more than the thing it protects?</strong> Some true sentences are not worth this week.</p>
</div>

Most of what raises your blood pressure fails all three. The discipline is not silence — it is choosing, and knowing that you chose.

Note:
This is the prescriptive centre of the session, and the one slide you should be willing to defend without hedging.

Run it live on something from the room. Take a current irritation and walk the three questions out loud. Failing all three is common, and it lands as a relief rather than an insult, because it converts a moral obligation into a decision.

Be careful with the third filter. It is not a licence to stay quiet about anything that costs you something; it is a test for the small stuff. The four things in session 3 fail all three filters as well, and that is exactly why they are worth the cost.

# The Work

<div class="hairline"></div>

*Fifteen minutes with your own week. Two columns you keep, one you argue with.*

Note:
Transition. The exercise is private, and it produces one decision rather than a plan.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Practice: the control sort" -->

## Practice: The Control Sort
### Everything that took energy this week

<div class="case">
<p><strong>Mine to decide.</strong> I choose, and the outcome is mine.</p>
<p><strong>Mine to influence.</strong> I can move it, and somebody else decides.</p>
<p><strong>Not mine.</strong> I have an opinion. That is all I have.</p>
</div>

Sort for eight minutes. Then pick the *one* item in column one you will act on, and the *one* in column three you will stop carrying. Say them to a partner.

Note:
The sorting is easy; the honesty is not. People leave items in column one because column one feels like responsibility. Watch for it and name it: claiming control you do not have is not diligence, it is a way of staying angry on purpose.

Column two is the interesting one and it is easy to skip. Influence is real work — a well-timed question, a review comment, a demo that shows the actual behaviour — and most of the available leverage lives there.

The two choices at the end are the whole exercise. One act, one release. Pairs get four minutes each.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Debrief" -->

## Debrief

<div class="questions">
<p>What were you carrying that you never decided to pick up?</p>
<p>Which item was hardest to file under "not mine" — and why?</p>
<p>What would change if you stopped working on it for a week?</p>
</div>

Note:
The first question tends to produce a long silence followed by something honest. Give it the silence.

The second question is a trap worth setting deliberately. The item that is hardest to file is almost always one where somebody else's opinion of you is at stake, which is not a control problem at all. Naming that distinction is useful: you cannot control the opinion, and you can control what you do next.

The third question is the carry-forward asked early. Let them answer it here, so next week's version is a report rather than a resolution.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="When not to spend it" -->

## When not to spend it
### The honest counterweight

Some hills are not worth the walk. Some environments cannot be fixed from where you sit. And leaving is a legitimate professional act, not a failure of stamina.

Water goes around the rock. It does not stay at the rock for years telling itself it is being principled.

Note:
Do not cut this slide. It is what keeps the session from becoming an argument for martyrdom, and somebody in the room needs to hear that the ground is not workable.

The distinction to draw: staying because you chose to is a position. Staying because leaving would feel like losing is a trap. The first has a line in it; the second does not.

You are not counselling anyone to leave, and say so, because in a small room that is how it will be heard. You are saying the option is real, and that a person who has never considered it is not choosing to stay — they are just still there.

## Decide in calm
### The pre-commitment

You will be asked, in a room, at short notice, to agree to something you do not believe. Nobody decides well in that moment.

So decide now: the sentence you will say, and the thing you will not agree to without thinking. Write both where you will find them.

Note:
The practical bridge to session 3. It converts a philosophy into preparation, which is what the room actually needs on a Tuesday.

Pre-commitment works because the hard part is recall under pressure, not courage. People who have rehearsed one sentence say it. People who have only decided to be brave do not.

Ask them to write the sentence, not the principle. "I'll need to check the estimate" is a sentence. "I'll be more careful about commitments" is not.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 2" data-foot="Carry forward" -->

## Carry Forward
### Act on one, release one

This week: do the one thing from column one, and stop working on the one thing from column three.

Both are needed. Acting without releasing is how the energy runs out; releasing without acting is how the calm turns into resignation.

Note:
Ask for both items to be written down and kept. Not collected, not shared with the group.

The pair is the point. Do only the first, and next week they report exhaustion. Do only the second, and next week they report that nothing changed and the session was naive. Naming that in advance makes the report honest either way.

Next session is the expensive one: saying the sentence out loud, to the person it concerns.

## Summary
### Three things to keep

<div class="questions">
<p><strong>Separate the kinds of control.</strong> Decide, influence, and adjacent are different jobs with different costs.</p>
<p><strong>Yielding is a choice about where force goes.</strong> It is not the same as going along with it.</p>
<p><strong>Decide in calm.</strong> The sentence you will need is not going to arrive under pressure.</p>
</div>

Note:
Read the three, stop, and let the room choose what to argue with. This is the session where disagreement is most useful, because the objections are the ones the next session has to answer.

If somebody says this is all just accepting the situation, do not defend the deck. Point at session 3 and say: hold that thought for two weeks, and see whether the four things we defend there are the things you would want accepted.
