## Since We Last Met
### A different question

Where did you pair this week, and what did the second person catch that you would have missed?

<div class="questions">
<p>What did pairing cost you — time, pride, the feeling of being fast?</p>
<p>Was it worth it on the days when nothing went wrong?</p>
</div>

**Pairing is about who is in the room. Today is about what the room asks of you.**

Note:
Give the group a few quiet moments before opening. Ask for one concrete exchange — one thing the second person caught, one thing it cost.

The honest answers will be about time and about pride. Name that out loud, because it sets up the session: the thing that makes pairing feel expensive is the same thing that makes telling the truth feel expensive. Both are social costs, not technical ones.

Keep this short. Three minutes. The bridge to today is the last line: pairing is about who is in the room, and today is about what the room asks of you.

## Reading Assignment
### Nothing to read. Deliberately.

<p class="lead">This is the only session in the series with no reading for attendees.</p>

Handing colleagues a reading list about their own moral distress turns the first session into homework about their problems — and the people who most need to be here are the least likely to do it.

Presenters read Festinger, *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*, ch. 1 and 4, and Katz & Kahn, *The Social Psychology of Organizations*, ch. 7.

Note:
Say the first part out loud, and say why. Do not apologise for the absence of homework; the absence is the design.

Those two chapters are where the vocabulary in this deck comes from, and they are worth your time, but do not put them in front of the room. Recognition is suppressed by preparation: a person who has read about their own situation arrives defensive, and the first hour is spent managing that instead of talking.

If someone asks what they can read afterwards, the honest answer is the deck for session 2, then the *Enchiridion*, which is an hour and is genuinely finishable.

# Ground Truth <small>The gap between the map and the ground</small>

<div class="hairline"></div>

Note:
Welcome to the topic. Two meanings of the phrase, and both are doing work today.

The surveyor's sense: ground truth is the actual state of the ground, as opposed to what the map says about it. The software sense: what the system actually does, as opposed to what the report says it does.

Neither of those implies lying. A map is not a lie; it is a summary made on a different day, by somebody who was not standing where you are standing. Everything in this session lives in that gap.

## Two Documents
### Same project. Different account.

<p class="lead">When did you last read something that described a project you were on, and recognise that it described a different project?</p>

Notice that the question was easy to answer. Notice how quickly you found the sentence.

Note:
No claim on this slide, on purpose. Ask the question, wait, and let the room answer it. Somebody will, and the answer will arrive faster than you expect, which is the point.

Do not ask what the document got wrong, and do not ask who wrote it. Ask only how far apart the two accounts were. The moment the room starts assigning blame, the session becomes a grievance meeting and you will not get it back.

If the room is quiet, offer a smaller version: a ticket closed as done where the thing is not done. Everyone has one.

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 1" data-foot="What vacation doesn't fix" -->

## This is not <em>burnout</em>.<br>Rest will not touch it.

<p class="lead">Burnout is too much of a thing you believe in. This is the exhaustion of doing work you don't believe, at a pace that is entirely reasonable.</p>

The remedy for the first is a holiday. The remedy for the second is a boundary, and no one has ever come back from two weeks away with one.

Note:
This is the slide that earns the session. If the room does not recognise itself here, stop and go back to the two documents.

The distinction matters because the remedies are different, and the wrong remedy costs a year. Burnout responds to rest, to workload, to control over your own time. This responds to none of those, which is why the holiday works for four days and the feeling is back by the second Tuesday.

There is a clinical term for the severe end of this — moral injury — and it is worth knowing and worth never using on anyone in the room. Offer it in conversation if it helps somebody name their own experience; never as a diagnosis. See the safety rails in the series README.

## Every role is a tug-of-war

<div class="tug">
<div class="tug-expectations">
<div class="tug-expectation">Ship it fast <span>legitimate</span></div>
<div class="tug-expectation">Keep everyone comfortable <span>legitimate</span></div>
<div class="tug-expectation">Keep it correct <span>legitimate</span></div>
</div>
<div class="tug-arrows"><div>&#8594;</div><div>&#8594;</div><div>&#8594;</div></div>
<div class="tug-person">One person,<br>one week</div>
</div>

The tension is a property of the role. It is not a defect in you.

Note:
Katz and Kahn's point, in chapter 7, is almost embarrassing in its plainness: a role is not a job description, it is a *set* of expectations, and the expectations contradict each other by design. Nobody sat down and designed the contradiction; it accumulates.

The word doing the work on those three cards is *legitimate*. Each of those expectations is a reasonable thing to want. That is what makes the situation hard, and it is also what makes it useless to look for the unreasonable person.

Say the last line and let it sit. For a lot of people in the room this is the first time anyone has told them the tension is not their fault, and it lands better without elaboration.

## Role Ambiguity
### The other way a role fails

Sometimes the expectations do not collide. Nobody has told you which one wins.

So you infer a priority from what gets praised, and you infer it silently, and you can be wrong for a year.

<div class="questions">
<p>Which priority in your current work has never actually been stated to you?</p>
</div>

Note:
This is the quieter half of chapter 7, and more day-to-day misery lives in it than in outright conflict. Conflict at least announces itself. Ambiguity does not: you find out in a review that you optimised for the wrong thing, and the correction arrives as a surprise.

The inference mechanism is worth naming. You watch what gets praised and what gets ignored, and you build a model of the real priorities. That model is usually accurate and occasionally catastrophic.

Ask the question, then leave a gap. Do not collect answers. The value is in the private recognition.

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 1" data-foot="Festinger, ch. 1 and 4" -->

## The group makes it worse

<div class="quote">
<p>Holding two conflicting beliefs at once is uncomfortable, and people will pay to stop.</p>
<cite>Festinger, <i>A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance</i>, ch. 1</cite>
</div>

The cheapest way to stop is agreement. Chapter 4 is the part people skip: a group that agrees on a story becomes *social support* for it, and dissent starts to feel like a personal defect rather than a difference of view.

Note:
Read the quote, then let the second half land slowly.

Festinger's contribution is not that people lie. It is that discomfort creates pressure, and pressure finds the cheapest exit. Changing your behaviour is expensive. Changing your belief is expensive. Agreeing with the room is free.

That is why a room of twelve decent people can converge on a story none of them believes: it is not hypocrisy, it is the normal resolution of an unpleasant state. Nobody in that room is a villain — which is exactly why the next slide exists.

## The cost of the smile
### Hochschild's surface acting

Faking a feeling you do not have, for long enough, is work. It is the kind of work that does not appear in a timesheet and does not stop when you go home.

The bill arrives as flatness: the meeting you cannot get interested in, the release you cannot get excited about.

Note:
One slide, one idea, and it is here because it explains the specific fatigue the room has been describing. Hochschild's distinction is between *deep* acting, where you change how you feel, and *surface* acting, where you only change the display. Surface acting is the expensive one.

Keep this at the level of work, not of anybody's mental health. This particular kind of labour has a cost, and the cost is real even when the workload is entirely fine.

If the room wants to sit here for ten minutes, let them. It is one of the few slides where the discussion is worth more than the content.

## Why structure, not villains
### The caveat, before we go further

Structural framing explains the pressure. It does not excuse your own conduct, and it is not a reason to stop caring.

It is what keeps you from moralising — and moralising is the failure mode that gets a person tuned out, after which nothing they say lands again.

Note:
Say this before the break, or the rest of the series reads as abdication. This is the slide that keeps the material honest.

The trap is subtle. Once you learn the tension is structural, the temptation is to conclude that nothing you do matters, or that everyone above you is compromised. Both conclusions are comfortable and both are useless. The structural lens exists to stop you blaming yourself; it does not exist to stop you deciding.

The second paragraph is the practical reason to care. Somebody who is angry all the time gets heard once. Somebody who is accurate all the time gets heard indefinitely, which is the only kind of hearing that changes anything.

## Practice: The Audit
### One page, two columns

<div class="case">
<p><strong>Column one.</strong> What the document says. Use its words, not yours.</p>
<p><strong>Column two.</strong> What you know to be true. Use yours.</p>
</div>

Eight minutes alone. Then find a partner and compare the *columns* — not the people, not the company.

Note:
Hand out paper or index cards. Do not let anyone use a shared document; the point is that this one is private.

The instruction to use the document's words in column one is deliberate. Paraphrasing early lets the two columns blur, and the exercise only works if you can see the distance. Ask them to copy a phrase verbatim.

When pairs compare, redirect any move toward "and that is why X is useless" back to column two: what do *you* know, and how would you say it. Six minutes for pairs is enough.

If someone cannot think of a document, offer the verbal version: the last status somebody gave you in a standup.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 1" data-foot="Debrief" -->

## Debrief

<div class="questions">
<p>What did you notice in column two that you had not said out loud before?</p>
<p>Which line was harder to write — the one about the work, or the one about yourself?</p>
<p>What would it cost to say one of those sentences in a meeting, this week?</p>
</div>

Note:
Take three or four answers, not ten. The third question is the one that matters; the first two are warm-ups.

Expect the third answer to be a social cost rather than a fear: "it would make me the person who says that." That is an accurate reading of the room, and it is worth naming as such. The second session is about what to do with that cost.

Do not resolve the exercise. It is a diagnostic, and diagnostics are allowed to leave people with a question.

## Carry Forward
### One sentence, to one person

This week, describe one thing accurately to one person who does not already know it.

Not a confrontation. Not a correction. A sentence that is true, said where it can be heard.

Bring what happened.

Note:
Be precise about the size of this. It is one sentence, said once, to somebody who is not already on your side. A note in a ticket counts. A comment in a review counts. A remark to a peer who will remember it counts.

The reason to keep it small: the first attempt is a test of the room, not of you. People discover the cost was lower than they expected, which is the belief the rest of the series depends on.

Ask them to bring the outcome, not the sentence. What happened is the material for session 2.
