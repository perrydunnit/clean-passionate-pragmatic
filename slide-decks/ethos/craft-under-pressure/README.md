# Craft Under Pressure

**Navigating Role Conflict and Competing Demands**

Three sessions about working inside expectations that are both legitimate and cannot both be met. The subject underneath the terms is simple: you are being asked to hold two things that do not fit, and neither of them is anybody's fault.

This is not a topic deck. There is no kata, because there is nothing to practise on code — the thing being practised happens in a meeting, so the exercise is the conversation. Each session still ends with a practice that produces something the participant keeps.

## The sessions

| # | Deck | The tension | The move |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | [Ground Truth](01-ground-truth/) | between what is true and what is said | be accurate in a place that persists |
| 2 | [What's Mine](02-whats-mine/) | between what you control and what you carry | the control sort; three filters for spending energy |
| 3 | [The Line](03-the-line/) | between keeping the peace and keeping your word | the four non-negotiables; one sentence, no adjectives |

Recognition, then calm, then agency. The order is not cosmetic: without session 1's naming, "what is up to you" is heard as fatalism.

## Reading

Session 1 asks nothing of attendees, deliberately. Handing colleagues a reading list about their own moral distress turns the first session into homework about their problems, and the people who most need to be there are the least likely to do it. The deck supplies the vocabulary instead.

Presenters read, in the order the sessions arrive:

- *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*, Festinger, ch. 1 and ch. 4; *The Social Psychology of Organizations*, Katz & Kahn, ch. 7 as reference. Katz & Kahn is several hundred pages of 1960s academic prose and is not a read-through.
- *Enchiridion*, Epictetus, in full (about an hour); *Tao Te Ching*, ch. 8, 22, 43, 78; *The Heart of the Buddha's Teaching*, ch. 9 and 12; *The Art of War*, ch. 1, 3, 10.
- *The Clean Coder*, ch. 1–3; *Extreme Programming Explained*, ch. 4–5.

Only *The Clean Coder* comes from the four books this series is compiled from. It covers *how to do the work*; it is nearly silent on what to do when the work is described dishonestly, and three chapters is the extent of it. The rest of the canon is elsewhere, and the README says so rather than implying otherwise.

## Before you run one

This subject can land hard, and a session about it can hurt someone in the room. Non-negotiable:

- **Never diagnose.** "Moral injury" is a clinical-adjacent word. Offer it; never tell anyone it describes them.
- **No disclosure round.** No "share a time this happened to you." In a room of ten colleagues, one of them is in the middle of it.
- **No named people, ever.** Redirect any turn toward a specific manager back to the structural version.
- **Signpost support**, on the last slide: if this is landing hard, that is worth talking about, and name the actual channel.
- **Confidentiality**, stated up front and kept: nothing said in the room appears in the write-up in a form anyone could attribute.

## Delivery

**Third person.** The decks describe the situation and the move; they do not narrate the presenter's own history. First person turns every claim into an anecdote, and this material works better as a description of a structural condition than as one person's account of surviving it.

**Read the notes.** Every slide's speaker notes carry the facilitation move — where the room will object, which slide they are most likely to misread, and what to do when they do.
