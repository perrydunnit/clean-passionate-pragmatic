## Since We Last Met
### Act on one, release one

<div class="questions">
<p>Which column-one item did you act on, and what happened?</p>
<p>What did you stop carrying — and did anything fall over?</p>
</div>

Two weeks of practice, and today is where it gets said to somebody's face.

Note:
Take the two questions in order. The second is the one people are shy about answering, because "nothing fell over" feels like a confession that they were carrying something pointless. It is actually the finding: most of what we carry is load-bearing only in our own heads.

If nobody acted, do not scold. Ask what stopped them, and write the answer down — it is usually the exact objection the scenarios today are designed to rehearse.

Keep this to ten minutes. Today's material is the only part of the series that is genuinely prescriptive, and it needs the time.

# The Line <small>Keeping the peace, and keeping your word</small>

<div class="hairline"></div>

Note:
Two sessions of diagnosis and calm. Today is the part that costs something.

Say up front that this is the prescriptive session and that you know it. Naming the change in register buys you the right to make strong claims for the next hour; not naming it makes the room feel ambushed.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Clean Coder, ch. 1" -->

## Professionalism is commitments
### Not attitude, not seniority

A professional is somebody who makes commitments they can keep, and tells the truth about the ones they cannot.

That is the whole test. It does not require brilliance, and it is not a personality type.

Note:
Martin's chapter 1 is mostly a list of behaviours, and the list is useful, but the underlying test is one sentence: can you be relied on to say what is true about what you will deliver?

The reason to frame it this way is that it removes the excuse. "I'm not senior enough to push back" is an answer about hierarchy; the commitment test does not care about hierarchy. Anyone who has promised a date is in the business of keeping it or reporting it.

Do not let this become a discussion about whether the industry is professional. It always does, and it is not the topic.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Clean Coder, ch. 2" -->

## Saying no
### A statement about arithmetic, not about a person

You are not refusing your manager. You are refusing an arithmetic that does not work, on behalf of the person who will be held to it.

The useful "no" is specific, has a reason, and offers the trade: *this is six weeks; I can give you three by cutting the migration.*

Note:
The reframe in the first line is the slide. Most people hear "no" as insubordination, and so they say yes to things that cannot happen, and then the failure arrives later and larger.

Martin's examples are worth walking through out loud because they are concrete and short. The pattern in all of them: state the fact, state the consequence, leave the decision with the person who owns it. That last part matters — a "no" that also decides for them is a different act and it will be resisted.

Push on the trade. A no with no alternative is a refusal; a no with a named trade is a decision the other person can make. Most of the time they take the trade, which is the point.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Clean Coder, ch. 3" -->

## Saying yes
### "I'll try" is a lie

Everybody in the room knows it is a lie, including the person saying it. The lie is what makes the next conversation impossible, because there is now a date that nobody believes and nobody can move.

Yes means: *I will do this by then, and I will tell you the moment that changes.*

Note:
This is the slide that quietly indicts everyone in the room, including you. Own that in the delivery — say where you have done it — or it reads as a lecture from somebody who has never been under pressure.

The mechanism is worth spelling out, because "I'll try" feels honest at the time. It transfers the risk to a future conversation that will be harder: the date is now in a plan, the plan is in a deck, and the person who said "I'll try" is the only one who knows it was never real.

The second half of the sentence is the part people forget. A yes is not a promise to succeed; it is a promise to report. That is what makes it survivable.

## Four things, and everything else

| Never negotiable | Always negotiable |
|---|---|
| Estimates I believe | How the work is sequenced |
| Truth about what is tested | Which approach we take |
| Whether something is broken | Whether we call it a hotfix |
| My name on the commit | Scope, dates, staffing, ambition |

The right column is enormous. That is the point: most of what feels like pressure to compromise is a negotiation you are allowed to lose.

Note:
This is the prescriptive centre of the whole series, and the one slide to defend without hedging.

The asymmetry is the argument. People resist boundaries because they imagine them as a wall across everything; the right column shows how much room is left once the left column is fixed. Four things is a number a person can hold on a bad day.

Take each row and ask the room for the objection. "I can't always be sure what is tested" is a real one and the answer is: then say what you are not sure about, which is itself the commitment.

The fourth row is the one that lands hardest in a room of engineers. Your name is on the commit. Whatever the org chart says about who decided, the diff has an author.

## The recorded decision
### The smallest professional act available

When a decision is made that you disagree with, do not re-litigate it. Get it written down, with a name and a date.

Then make it a habit rather than an act of courage: small loops, visible trade-offs, demos that show what actually works. Beck's values and principles are a system for telling the truth by default.

Note:
Two ideas on one slide, and they belong together because they solve the same problem from opposite ends.

The recorded decision is the individual move, and it is the lowest-risk professional act there is. "We are shipping with these three known issues; the decision was made on the 14th by X" is not insubordination — it is diligence. It also protects the person who made the call, which is why it is rarely resisted.

The systemic move is Beck's: if demos show real behaviour and trade-offs are visible every week, then truth does not depend on anyone's courage. That is the difference between a team where one person keeps saying the uncomfortable thing and a team where nobody has to.

# The Scenarios

<div class="hairline"></div>

*Three passes per scenario: name the conflict, separate control, state the boundary.*

Note:
Set the format before the first scenario, and keep it identical for all three so the room is not learning two things at once. Three passes: name the conflict in structural language, work out which part is yours to decide, then say the boundary to a partner out loud.

Pairs. Three minutes per pass. Swap roles so both people say a sentence.

The partner's job is to be the other party, not to be agreeable. Say that explicitly: a partner who nods is not useful.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Scenario A" -->

## Scenario A
### The report that isn't true

<div class="case">
<p>A status update is due. One integration is unbuilt and the demo last week used a stub. The draft says <strong>on track</strong>.</p>
<p>You are not the author. You have been asked to review it.</p>
</div>

Name the conflict. Separate control. Then say the sentence, to a partner, out loud.

Note:
The interesting failure here is that the room wants to talk about the author. Redirect: what do *you* write, in the review, and when.

Notice how much room the vocabulary gives you. "The integration is stubbed as of last Thursday; the demo exercised the stub" is a factual sentence that changes the document without accusing anyone of anything. That is the whole technique, and it takes practice to produce under pressure.

Let the partner push back — "that reads as undermining me" — and see what the person does with it. Most will soften into vagueness on the second attempt, which is the failure to name out loud.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Scenario B" -->

## Scenario B
### The estimate you're told to halve

<div class="case">
<p>You estimate six weeks. It comes back with: leadership needs it in three.</p>
<p>Nothing about the work has changed.</p>
</div>

Three passes. The failure to watch for is agreeing to three while privately planning six.

Note:
That failure is the most common and least discussed unprofessional act in the industry, and it is in *The Clean Coder* almost verbatim. Name it before the pairs start, because if you do not, half the room will rehearse it and believe they have been pragmatic.

The sentence to practise is the trade, not the refusal: "Six weeks for all of it. Three if we drop the migration. Which do you want?" That leaves the decision where it belongs and makes the cost visible.

If a pair produces a flat no with no trade, that is worth working on — it is a refusal, and it will be overruled rather than heard.

---

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Scenario C" -->

## Scenario C
### The thing you're asked to ship silently

<div class="case">
<p>A known failure path, in a path customers use. The release note does not mention it.</p>
<p>Somebody senior has decided this is acceptable, and has not written that decision down anywhere.</p>
</div>

Run this one only if the first two went well. The boundary is rarely "refuse".

Note:
The hardest scenario, and the one closest to the room's actual situation. It is deliberately last and deliberately optional — if A and B are still generating discussion, stop there and keep this in reserve.

The move is almost never refusal. It is: get the decision recorded, with a name and a date, and make sure the support path knows. "We are shipping with this known issue" is a different sentence from "this is fine", and it is usually enough.

Watch for the person who wants to be a hero here. The hero refuses, is overruled, and then has no record and no influence. The recorded decision is the less romantic move and the one that survives.

## One Sentence
### No adjectives, no apology

<div class="case">
<p>"The release will ship with three known crash paths in checkout. I recommend we delay the announcement a week and fix two of them."</p>
<p>"The work is six weeks. I can give you three by cutting the migration, and that is a decision I need you to make."</p>
</div>

Two sentences, no hedging, no "just", no "sorry". Rewrite yours until both are true.

Note:
The constraint is the exercise: no adjectives, no apology, no softening words. "Just", "quickly", "I think maybe" — all banned for the duration. What is left is usually a sentence that somebody can act on.

Have each pair say their sentence twice. The second time is always better, and the improvement is usually a deleted word rather than an added one.

Ask for two or three volunteers to say theirs to the room. This is the moment the session earns its name, and hearing a peer say a clean sentence is worth more than any amount of your explanation.

## Debrief

<div class="questions">
<p>What did saying it cost — and was the cost in the room, or in your head beforehand?</p>
<p>Where did you want to soften the sentence, and what word were you reaching for?</p>
<p>Which of the four things would you defend, and which would you trade?</p>
</div>

Note:
The second question is the sharpest. The word people reach for is almost always a hedge, and the hedge is doing a specific job: it is buying permission to have said it. Naming that impulse is more useful than criticising it.

The third question sorts the room honestly. Somebody will trade "whether something is broken" and that is worth exploring rather than correcting — it is usually a story about a manager they trust.

Do not resolve. This session ends with a written commitment, not a conclusion.

## Leaving is legitimate
### The counterweight

Some environments cannot be fixed from where you sit. Staying because you chose to is a position; staying because leaving would feel like losing is a trap.

A boundary you are not allowed to act on is not a boundary. It is a performance.

Note:
Say this with the same conviction as the four things, or the series becomes an argument for endurance, and endurance is not a virtue when the ground is not workable.

The distinction in the first paragraph is the useful part. A chosen stay has a line in it — there is something you would not do, and you know what it is. An unchosen stay has no line, because the alternative was never on the table.

You are not recommending anything. You are making sure the option is visible, because a person who has never considered leaving is not choosing to stay.

<!-- .slide: data-kicker="Craft Under Pressure · Session 3" data-foot="Carry forward" -->

## Carry Forward
### One sentence, two weeks

Write the sentence. Write the date you will say it. Keep both where you will see them.

Not collected, not shared, not checked. The commitment is to yourself, which is also the only way this session stays out of the disclosure business.

Note:
Two weeks from now is the horizon: long enough to find the moment, short enough that the sentence is still the one they wrote.

Say explicitly that nothing is collected. In a small in-person group the instinct is to make this a public commitment, and public commitments turn a professional boundary into a performance for colleagues.

Mention the write-up here — that a piece is being written from what the room raised, that nothing will be attributable, and that this is the reason there was no pre-read. Then keep that promise.
